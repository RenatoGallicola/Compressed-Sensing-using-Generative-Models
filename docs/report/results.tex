\section{Results}
\vspace{1cm}
We created two versions of both VAE and DCGAN using different lengths for the latent representation vector \( z \). In one case, we used a latent vector of dimension 20, and in the other, a dimension of 30. Together with the LASSO baseline this gives five methods to compare.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}

Every method is evaluated on the same ten test images, one for each digit class, so that the comparison is not driven by a single lucky or unlucky example. At each measurement budget \( M \) the five methods receive the same measurement matrix \( A \) and the same noise vector, and the noise is scaled so that its expected norm remains equal to 0.1 whatever the value of \( M \).
\\
The reconstruction quality is measured as the squared distance between the reconstruction and the original image, divided by the number of pixels:
\begin{equation}
\text{error per pixel} = \frac{\lVert \hat{x} - x^* \rVert_2^2}{n}, \qquad n = 784 .
\end{equation}
This is not the same quantity as the measurement residual \( \lVert AG(\hat{z}) - y \rVert \) that the recovery algorithm minimizes. The residual decreases as \( M \) decreases, simply because fewer constraints have to be satisfied, so it describes how well the optimization converged rather than how good the reconstruction is. For this reason it is used only as a diagnostic, and every number reported below is computed against the ground truth image.
\\
All the reconstructions obtained with a generative prior use a thousand gradient steps and ten random restarts, keeping the restart with the smallest measurement residual.

\subsection{Baseline Performance Using LASSO}

We started by evaluating the baseline performance using LASSO for reconstruction. LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) assumes sparsity in the signal and solves the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = \arg \min_x \|y - Ax\|_2^2 + \lambda \|x\|_1,
\end{equation}
\\
where \( y \) is the measurement vector, \( A \) is the measurement matrix, and \( \lambda \) is a regularization parameter. We solve it in an orthonormal DCT basis, which is the domain in which MNIST digits are compressible.
\\
From our experiments, we observed that the performance of LASSO is inadequate for this task until a substantial number of measurements is available. Below 100 measurements the reconstruction is dominated by background noise, and a coherent digit only emerges from about 200 measurements onwards. On the other hand LASSO keeps improving as measurements are added, and it is the most accurate method of all once the budget is large, as discussed below.

Three regimes can be distinguished.
\\\\
\textbf{Few measurements.} Up to about one hundred measurements the generative priors are far more accurate than LASSO. With 25 measurements the VAE with latent dimension 30 has an error of 0.0250 against 0.1049 for LASSO, that is 4.2 times lower, and at that budget the LASSO reconstruction is not recognizable as a digit at all. This is the regime that motivates compressed sensing, and it is where the choice of the prior matters most.
\\\\
\textbf{Sample efficiency.} LASSO needs 400 measurements to reach an error of 0.0117. The VAE with latent dimension 30 reaches the same level with 75 measurements, a saving of a factor 5.3, and the DCGAN with latent dimension 20 with 200 measurements, a factor 2. Bora et al. \cite{bora2017} report a saving between 5 and 10 times for their models, so our best model sits at the lower end of the range they describe.
\\\\
\textbf{Many measurements.} From 500 measurements onwards LASSO becomes the most accurate method and keeps improving, reaching 0.0015 with 750 measurements against 0.0067 for the best generative model, a reversal by a factor 4.4. This behaviour is expected and is described in \cite{bora2017}, where the authors observe that LASSO eventually surpasses the generative approach and that this requires more than 500 measurements. Our experiment reproduces that threshold.
\\\\
The reason for the reversal is that the reconstruction is constrained to lie in the range of the generator. The distance between a real digit and that range does not depend on the number of measurements, so once enough measurements are available to identify the closest point in the range, additional measurements bring no benefit. Averaging the budgets from 200 upwards, this residual error settles at 0.0068 for the VAE with latent dimension 30, 0.0100 for the DCGAN with latent dimension 20, 0.0204 for the DCGAN with latent dimension 30 and 0.0286 for the VAE with latent dimension 20. These four values are properties of the generators and not of the recovery algorithm.

\subsection{Reconstruction Examples}

Figure \ref{fig:reconstruction grid} shows the same digit reconstructed by every method at every budget.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{assets/reconstruction_grid.png}
    \caption{One test digit reconstructed by the five methods as the number of measurements increases}
    \label{fig:reconstruction grid}
\end{figure}

The visual comparison confirms the numbers. LASSO returns noise until about 200 measurements. The VAE with latent dimension 20 produces a plausible digit almost immediately, but it never sharpens, and even with 750 measurements it returns a shape halfway between a three and a five. The DCGAN with latent dimension 20 gives a sharp digit from 25 measurements onwards, which is consistent with the fact that an adversarial generator is trained to produce samples that a discriminator accepts, rather than an average of the plausible reconstructions as the VAE does.

\subsection{The Latent Dimension}

For the VAE the latent dimension makes a clear difference. The version with \( k = 30 \) is more accurate than the one with \( k = 20 \) at every budget from 50 upwards, and the gap ranges between 2.6 and 4.7 standard errors, so it is not attributable to the choice of the test images. A larger latent space describes the digits more faithfully and therefore lowers the error floor discussed above.
\\
For the DCGAN the situation is less clear. The version with \( k = 20 \) is ahead from 75 measurements onwards and the sign of the difference is consistent, but over ten test images the gap never exceeds 1.9 standard errors. We therefore report it as a trend and not as a conclusion, since establishing it would require a larger set of test images.

\subsection{Computational Cost}

The five methods are not comparable in cost. Reconstructing the ten test images at one budget, with ten restarts and a thousand gradient steps, takes on average 1.4 seconds with LASSO, about 20 seconds with a VAE decoder and about 790 seconds with a DCGAN generator. The factor 38 between the two generative priors is explained by the size of the generator, since the DCGAN generator ends with a transposed convolution producing 512 feature maps at full resolution. The complete experiment reported in this section required 4.5 hours of computation.
\\
This cost is incurred at every reconstruction and not only once during training, because recovery is itself an optimization problem. In an application where measurements are cheap and reconstructions are frequent, this is on its own a decisive argument in favour of LASSO.
