\section{Conclusions}
\vspace{1cm}
This project assessed the performance of compressed sensing with generative priors on the MNIST dataset, comparing a VAE and a DCGAN of our own design, the fully connected VAE of the reference paper, and LASSO in two sparsifying bases as the classical baseline.
\\\\
\textbf{Key Findings:}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The result of the reference paper is reproduced:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Every VAE prior reaches with 75 measurements the accuracy either LASSO baseline requires 400 measurements to obtain, a saving of a factor 5.3. The factor is the same against both baselines, so it does not rest on a choice of reference. The authors report a saving between 5 and 10, so our reproduction falls at the lower end of the interval they publish.
        \item From 500 measurements onwards both LASSO baselines have a lower mean error than every generative prior, and at 750 the difference is significant for every pair. The paper describes the same reversal and states that it requires more than 500 measurements. In the pixel basis it is dramatic: with 750 measurements LASSO recovers the digits almost exactly while the generative priors remain on their floors.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The advantage has a ceiling, and the ceiling is the generator:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The accuracy of the generative priors stops improving after roughly 200 measurements, because the reconstruction is constrained to the range of the generator and the distance between a real digit and that range does not depend on the number of measurements.
        \item Averaged over the budgets from 300 upwards, that floor ranges from 0.0070 for the best model to 0.0235 for the worst, a factor 3.4 between priors, so the choice of generator matters far more than any refinement of the recovery algorithm in this regime.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The comparison among the VAE priors is not resolved by this experiment:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The fully connected network of the reference paper, our convolutional VAE with \( k = 20 \) and our convolutional VAE with \( k = 30 \) are statistically indistinguishable. With ten test images no comparison among the three reaches significance at any budget once corrected, and their error floors span 0.0006. We report this as an absence of evidence rather than as a ranking, and we note that we had expected to be able to order them.
        \item The DCGAN is the least accurate prior at almost every budget and by far the most expensive, about 103 times the cost of the fully connected decoder per reconstruction. On this dataset there is no trade-off to arbitrate: the cheapest generative prior is also among the most accurate ones. Its two columns should be read with the caveat recorded in section 6: those generators saw the test split during training and predate the current training recipe.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The training procedure is part of the result:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Repeating the same VAE training with different random seeds produced generators whose quality varied by a factor of 3.5, comparable to the entire spread between the priors we set out to compare. The cause was partial posterior collapse.
        \item Adding a KL warm-up removed the instability and improved every model. Any comparison between priors is only meaningful once it is larger than this variability, which is why the conclusions above are supported by paired tests rather than by single runs.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Future Directions:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reducing the representation error:} the ceiling described above is a property of the generator, so the way to improve the method in the high measurement regime is a more expressive generative model rather than a better recovery algorithm.
    \item \textbf{Separating the VAE priors:} ten test images are not enough to tell the three of them apart, so the natural next step is simply a larger evaluation set. That costs recovery time rather than new models, and it would settle whether the architecture and the latent dimension matter at all on this task.
    \item \textbf{A budget dependent regularizer:} our sweep shows that the value of \( \lambda \) that minimizes the error decreases as measurements are added, from 1 at the smallest budgets to 0 at the largest, so a schedule rather than a constant is the natural refinement.
    \item \textbf{Optimizing measurement matrices:} designing or learning matrices adapted to a specific dataset, keeping in mind that the theoretical guarantee relies on the randomness of \( A \) and would be traded away.
    \item \textbf{Applications beyond MNIST:} MNIST digits form a simple and low dimensional manifold. Applying the method to more complex datasets would test whether the advantage in the low measurement regime survives.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.01cm}
In conclusion, combining a generative prior with compressed sensing is worthwhile exactly when measurements are the scarce resource, which is the situation compressed sensing was designed for. On MNIST that regime ends well before the signal dimension is reached, and past it a classical sparsity assumption is not merely competitive but far better. The gain is bounded by how well the generator represents the data, and it is paid for with a reconstruction that is orders of magnitude more expensive than a convex solve. Both facts have to be weighed in applications such as medical imaging, where a single measurement can be slow, costly or harmful to the patient, and where the reconstruction happens once.
